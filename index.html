Section A: The Big Idea

Evidence turns a complaint into a case

Last week you worked out who you are convincing and what they care about. This week you go get proof.

Picture Dani walking up to Ray, the shop owner, and saying, "The lift is always busy." Ray does not say yes. Ray says, "How do you know?"

That question is the whole week. A complaint is something you feel. A case is something you can show. Ray cannot approve a $5,300 machine because Dani is frustrated. He can approve it if Dani shows him that the problem is real, that it keeps happening, what it costs, and what other people who have dealt with it say.

The three jobs this week

  1. Lock in your target. Pull your ask and your problem over from Week 1, so everything you gather has something to prove.
  2. Name the pattern. Figure out the real pattern behind your problem and find out what it is actually called.
  3. Gather evidence in three rounds. Round One proves the problem is recognized. Round Two is hard numbers. Round Three is real voices from people who have used this kind of equipment.

Two rules for this week

  • Every source is real and has a working link. If you cannot link to it, you cannot use it.
  • Nothing is generated for you. You do the thinking and the searching. AI can help you search, but it cannot be your source. No AI-made sources, no made-up quotes, no summaries written by a tool.

Dani keeps going through every section as the example. Dani is made up. The sources Dani uses are real. Your own work will use your own field and your own facts.

This interactive reading explains what each part of the Week 2 worksheet is asking and what a strong answer looks like. You do the actual research on the worksheet. Fill the worksheet out as you really do the research, not after.

Where this goes: Your research this week feeds the Introduction, the Review of Literature, and the Methodology. Your Conclusion is still yours to write, in the Canvas quiz.

Section B: Step 1

Lock in your target

Step 1 on the Week 2 Research Worksheet is where you bring two things over from Week 1: your one-sentence ask and your problem with the incident that proves it. Open your Week 1 Word doc and copy them from there.

Why bother? Because this is your target. Every source you gather this week has to help prove this specific ask. If you cannot see how a source connects back to the target, it does not belong in your research, no matter how interesting it is.

Watch it work: Dani, Step 1

Ask: "I am asking you to approve buying a second lift, around $5,300, so the shop is never down to one lift."

Problem: "Last Tuesday, in the first rush of tire season, our only lift was down for six hours. We turned away four jobs at about $160 each, roughly $640 in one day."

While searching, Dani finds a good article about how tire pressure sensors work on electric vehicles. It is interesting. But it says nothing about a shop running out of lift time, so it does not serve the target. Dani leaves it out.

Why it works: the target is specific enough that Dani can test every source against it. A vague target lets everything in.

Doing this well

  • Copy your ask and your problem exactly as you wrote them in Week 1.
  • Keep the ask to one sentence that names the exact item and the decision you need.
  • If your Week 1 ask was vague, like "better equipment," tighten it now. A vague target gets vague research.
  • Keep the number from your incident. You will need it again later.
  • If you work in your industry, use a real incident. If you do not have experience yet, imagine a realistic one in your industry.
Where this goes: Your target is the filter for everything else this week. Each source has to serve this ask.

Quick Concept

What is a theoretical framework, and why does it matter?

A theoretical framework is the named pattern behind your problem. It is the idea that explains why your problem is real, why it keeps happening, and why your ask makes sense.

Why it matters

  • It turns a complaint into a pattern. Without one, a supervisor can wave off each incident as a bad day or a rare fluke. With one, the incidents become a single problem that is real and ongoing.
  • It gives your proposal a backbone. The framework shapes how you present your evidence. It tells the reader what to look for in everything that follows.
  • It shows you did your homework. You did not make up a reason. You found a real concept, from a real source, that fits your facts.
  • It makes you harder to argue with. When someone asks "why do you think this is the cause?", you have a named idea and a source to point to.
Watch it work: Dani

Without a framework: "The lift is always busy."

With a framework: "This is a bottleneck. According to the Theory of Constraints, a system can only produce as much as its slowest step allows. Our one lift is the slowest step."

Why it works: the second version names the pattern and points to a source, so Ray has something to evaluate instead of a feeling to dismiss.

Where it shows up

It becomes the Theoretical Framework section of your proposal. It also shapes your Review of Literature and the way the rest of your research is organized.

Where this goes: Everything you gather in Rounds One to Three exists to prove this specific pattern is real.

Section C: Step 2, Part 1

Find the pattern yourself

Step 2 builds your theoretical framework. The Quick Concept page explained what it is and why it matters. Now you start building yours.

Part 1 is where you find that pattern yourself, before you look anything up. The worksheet asks four questions.

  1. What is actually changing on the work side? Think about the work itself: job length, volume, materials. Not the symptom you are seeing.
  2. What has stayed exactly the same on the equipment side?
  3. Did this build up over time, or hit all at once? How do you know? Point to a specific moment, not a feeling that it got worse.
  4. If nothing changes, does this resolve on its own, or does it keep happening? Why?
Watch it work: Dani, Step 2 Part 1

Work side: "Winter tire season starts in October and our work doubles. More customers come in for tire swaps and seasonal checks, and every one of those jobs goes through the same lift."

Equipment side: "We still have one 15-year-old lift. It takes about 50 minutes per job and is booked about six hours a day. Nothing about the lift, the bays, or the way we run a job has changed. Only the number of cars has. That has not changed."

Built up or all at once: "It builds every season. Last year we were backed up almost every Saturday from October to January, and the backlog never fully cleared until the weather turned."

Resolve on its own? "No. The lift does not gain capacity between busy weeks, so the next busy week overflows the same way. Unless something about the lift changes, this will happen again every winter."

Why it works: Dani points to real moments and numbers, and the answers line up into one picture. The work grew and the equipment did not.
Symptom (weak)"The lift keeps going down."
Change in the work (strong)"Jobs double every winter, and the lift stays the same size."

Doing this well

  • Describe a change in the work itself, not just the thing that annoys you.
  • Name what stayed fixed on the equipment side, with numbers if you can.
  • Point to a specific moment when you or others noticed it.
  • Explain whether it keeps happening and why.
Where this goes: This does not get its own section in the paper. It sets up the pattern that Part 2 gives a name to, and that your Review of Literature explains.

Section D: Step 2, Part 2

Name the concept

Part 2 asks you to go find out what your pattern is actually called. This named pattern becomes the Theoretical Framework section of your research proposal. It is the idea behind your whole argument, and it shapes how you present your evidence. This is a real concept, found in a textbook, a course reading, a credible article, or a professional field. You are naming a concept. You are not inventing one.

How to find your framework

Follow these steps in order. Use your own pattern and problem each time.

  1. Start with your pattern. Open your notes from Section C. Write your pattern as one short phrase. For Dani: "demand grows but one machine stays the same."
  2. Pull out the key words. Circle the two or three words that carry the idea, like "demand," "one machine," or "slows." These are your search words.
  3. Add a framework word and search. Take your key words and add one of these words: theory, model, framework, or concept. Then type it into a search bar. Try two or three versions.
    Watch it work: Dani. Dani's key words are demand, one machine, and slows. Dani tries:
    • one machine slows production theory
    • demand grows limited capacity model
    • why does one machine slow down a whole shop framework
    Dani's second search leads to a page about the Theory of Constraints. That is the name Dani was looking for.
    Your turn: Write your key words, add theory, model, or framework, and search.
  4. Ask AI for leads, not answers. Ask: "What established theories or models explain [your pattern] in [your workplace]?" AI gives you names to search. It is not your source. Take each name and find where it really comes from.
    Watch it work: Dani. "What established theories or models explain [demand outgrowing one machine] in [a car repair shop]?"
    Your turn: Fill in the blanks with your own pattern and workplace.
  5. Find the real source. Look for a textbook, a course reading, a university page, or a professional or trade organization. Your ISU library search and Google Scholar also work. Follow the page back to the real person, book, or field.
    Start here: ISU Library: Find Articles or Google Scholar
  6. Test the fit. Finish this sentence: "[Concept] says ____, and that explains why ____ at my workplace." If you cannot finish it, the concept does not fit. Go back to step 3 and try another.
    Watch it work: Dani. "[The Theory of Constraints] says [a system can only produce as much as its slowest step allows], and that explains why [our shop can only finish as many jobs as the one lift allows]."
    Your turn: Finish the sentence with your own concept and workplace.
  7. Save it. Save the link now by pasting it into the notes box below. That way you can copy and paste it into your worksheet later. Read the source well enough to explain it in your own words. If your supervisor asked "where did you get that?", you should be able to say who it is from, what it says, and why you trust them.

Look at two or three possible frameworks before you choose. The first one you find is rarely the best fit.

The five answers the worksheet wants

  1. The name of the concept.
  2. Where it comes from. A researcher, field, textbook, or course material you would trust. On the worksheet this has four boxes: the source title and author or publisher, the link, where you found it, and what kind of source it is. You should be able to explain what it says and why you trust who wrote it.
  3. Why it fits your case. Use your own facts from Part 1.
  4. Another way to read the same facts, and why you ruled it out.
  5. What someone would have to show you to prove the framework does not apply.
Watch it work: Dani, Step 2 Part 2

Concept: "Theory of Constraints."

Source title: "Theory of Constraints (TOC) | Lean Production," Vorne Industries.

Link: https://leanproduction.com/theory-of-constraints

Where found: "My second search, 'bottleneck one machine slows production,' led to this page."

Kind of source: "A company's lean production training page. The company may want to sell its products, so it is not a scholarly source, but it names the concept clearly. I use it to name the idea, not to prove my case."

What it says: "Every process has a single constraint, and total process throughput can only be improved when the constraint is improved."

Why it fits: "Our one lift is the slowest step. Every job has to pass through it, so the whole shop can only finish as many jobs as the lift allows. The techs, the parts, and the customers are all ready, and everything waits on one machine. That is exactly what the concept describes."

Another reading: "You could call this a staffing problem. I ruled it out because our techs are already standing around waiting for the lift, so more people would not fix it."

What would prove it wrong: "If the lift sat empty for hours each day and jobs still stacked up, the lift would not be the real limit. If the jobs we turn away were waiting on parts or techs instead of the lift, the concept would not apply."

Why it works: Dani names a real concept, links to a real source, ties it to real facts from the shop, and says honestly what would break the idea.
If you cannot name a real researcher, field, or source your concept traces back to, you have not found the real concept yet. You have made one up. Keep looking.
Where this goes: This is the Theoretical Framework. It shapes your Review of Literature and the way the rest of your research is organized.

Quick Concept

Three kinds of sources

Before you go find sources, you need to know what each round is looking for. There are three kinds, one for each round.

1. Sources that describe the problem (Round One)

  • What they are: Industry guidance, trade articles, and reports that describe the problem and how people in the industry deal with it or get past it.
  • Dani: [A trade association article on how shops plan for the seasonal rush.]
  • Why it matters: It proves the problem is more than one person's complaint, and it shows what others do about it.

2. Quantitative sources (Round Two)

  • What they are: Numbers. This is where you will find spec sheets for different products. Cost, speed, capacity, and warranty length are all things you can measure and compare.
  • Dani: ["The BendPak XPR-10AS takes 45 seconds to full rise."] Dani found this on a manufacturer spec sheet.
  • Why it matters: Numbers show the size of the problem and the difference each product makes. Spec sheets are the seller's own numbers, so look for a second source to check them.

3. Qualitative sources (Round Three)

  • What they are: People's experiences. What people say, describe, or report from using the equipment or living with the problem. Reviews, forum posts, interviews.
  • Dani: A shop owner wrote in Fleet Maintenance magazine: ["I've used this lift every day for the last two years and it's never let me down"]
  • Why it matters: Voices show what it is actually like to live with, which numbers cannot.

Why you need all three

Each one answers a question your supervisor will ask. The problem sources answer "Is this really a problem?" The numbers answer "How much will it help?" The voices answer "Will it work for people like us?" If one is missing, there is a gap in your case.

Where this goes: Round One finds the problem sources, Round Two finds the quantitative sources, and Round Three finds the qualitative sources. Knowing the difference tells you what to look for in each.

Quick Concept

How to search for sources

Dani has the framework name, the ask, and a list of what each round needs. Now Dani has to find the sources. Searching is a skill, and the first page of results is rarely the best one.

  1. Start with your own words. Use the framework name, the problem, or the exact product. Dani's first searches were "Theory of Constraints" and "BendPak XPR-10AS spec sheet."
  2. Add the kind of source you want. Put "spec sheet," "cutsheet," "review," or "forum" next to the topic. Round Two wants numbers, so ask for numbers. Round Three wants people, so ask for people. Dani searched "BendPak XPR-10AS owner review" and landed on a trade magazine review. For the other brand, Dani searched "Rotary vs Bendpak two post lift" and landed on a forum thread.
  3. Use quotation marks for exact phrases. "Theory of constraints" only brings up pages with those words together.
  4. Fix weak results one change at a time. Too many results: add a word. Too few: drop one. Change one thing so you know what helped.
  5. Round One: start with trade associations and the ISU Library. Industry guidance usually lives on trade association sites and in library databases. If you get stuck, a librarian will help you search.
  6. Round Two: go to the maker first, then a retailer. The maker's spec sheet has the specs. A retailer page often has the price and warranty the sheet leaves out. Dani used both for the BendPak.
  7. Judge it before you save it. This takes about 10 seconds. Who published it? Is a person or an organization named? Is there a date or version? Does it connect to your problem? If you cannot tell who wrote it, skip it. Dani skipped a blog post because no person was named.
Where this goes: Good searches save you time in Rounds One, Two, and Three, and a quick check before you save a source keeps weak ones out of your list.

Section E: Step 3

Round One: finding the problem

Round One has one job: find sources that show your problem is a recognized issue in your industry, not something you are imagining. You are not looking for the product yet. This week you find and save the sources. In Week 3 you open each one and dig into what it says.

Use your framework to guide your search

Your framework is the lens for everything you find from here on. Pull the key words from your framework and pattern, and use them in your searches. A source does not have to name your framework. It just has to be about the same kind of problem.

Watch it work: Dani

Dani's framework is the Theory of Constraints. Dani searches [bottleneck], [limited capacity], and [auto repair shop]. A trade association page on planning for seasonal demand never says "Theory of Constraints," but it is about the same problem. It fits.

Think about it from Ray's side. "Dani says the lift is a bottleneck" is one person's view. "A trade association publishes guidance on planning for seasonal demand" is the industry saying it is a real thing. That turns a shop complaint into a documented necessity.

Why this round matters

Your supervisor will ask one question first: is this a real problem, or just your opinion? These sources are your answer. Each one shows that people outside your workplace recognize the same problem. They become the proof behind your proposal, so the quality of what you save here matters.

What counts here

  • Strong: regulations, standards bodies, trade association guidance, industry reports, and professional training materials.
  • Weak: a page where no person is named, or where you cannot find out what the writer does in the trade. You cannot tell who is speaking or whether they know the work.

How to find your sources

  1. Write your problem in one sentence. Use your notes from earlier. Dani: [one lift limits how many jobs the shop can finish].
  2. Ask who would write about it. Who in your industry cares about this problem? Trade associations, trade magazines and journals, certification and training bodies, equipment makers, and safety or licensing agencies all publish on problems like this. Dani lists an automotive service trade association and a trade magazine for repair shops.
  3. Search with a source word added. Take your framework and problem key words and add guidance, standard, report, or best practices. Dani tries [bottleneck auto repair shop capacity] guidance.
    • Look where your trade talks. Search your trade association, trade magazines, and certification or training bodies by name.
    • Check for articles and reports too. Google Scholar and the ISU Library may turn up something useful. If you find little, that is normal for a trade, so keep going with the trade sources.
    • If you use AI, check every link yourself. AI sometimes makes up links. Click each one and make sure it opens a real page that says what you expect.
  4. Do a quick check before you save. Who wrote it, and what do they do in the trade? Is it recent? Does it explain the problem? Use the list above.
  5. Save the link now. Paste it into your notes right away so you can copy it into the worksheet later. Save a few extra options so you can choose the best ones.

What the worksheet asks for each source

  • The title and publisher.
  • A working link.
  • Where you found it.
  • What kind of source it is.

You need at least two sources, and one more answer: what else did you look at and rule out, and why did these win? That shows you compared real options and did not just click the first two results. If you find more, the worksheet has a button to add more sources.

Watch it work: Dani, Step 3

Source 1: "Tire Shop Spring Preparation: Gear Up for the Busy Season," by Matt White, Director of Tire Services at the Tire Industry Association, published on Autosphere.

Link: https://autosphere.ca/tires/2026/05/06/tire-shop-spring-preparation-gear-up-for-the-busy-season/

Where found: "I searched 'planning for seasonal demand tire shops' and followed a result to a trade magazine article."

Kind of source: "Trade association article."

Source 2: "Preparing and Planning for Winter," a column by Diane Freeman, Executive Director of the Automotive Aftermarket Retailers of Ontario, published on Autosphere.

Link: https://autosphere.ca/mechanical/2021/09/16/preparing-and-planning-for-winter/

Where found: "I searched 'preparing tire shop seasonal changeover' and followed a result to a column on the same trade site."

Kind of source: "Trade association column. The author is the Executive Director of the Automotive Aftermarket Retailers of Ontario, and her group gains members, not a sale from my shop."

Ruled out: "A tire shop blog post about busy seasons. I skipped it because no person was named, so I could not tell who wrote it or what they do in the trade. I would rather have two named sources than three where one has no author."

Why it works: both sources are independent of any seller and come from people who speak for the trade. Dani can say exactly where each was found, and the rule-out shows Dani looked at more than two results. Dani will dig into what the articles say in Week 3.

Doing this well

  • Get at least two real sources, each with a working link.
  • Note where you found each one and what kind of source it is.
  • Pick sources that connect to the pattern from Step 2.
  • Name at least one source you looked at and ruled out, and say why.
Where this goes: Round One sources and your framework feed the Review of Literature in your final proposal.

Section F: Step 4

Round Two: finding the numbers

Round Two is quantitative data. That means numbers: cost, specs, capacity, speed, warranty length, anything you can measure about the equipment. This round is strictly numbers. Opinions and reviews wait for Round Three. Round Two compares two different brands of the same type of product, like two two-post lifts from two makers.

This week you find at least two options and the sources that have their numbers. You are not pulling the numbers out yet. In Week 3 you open each source, put the numbers side by side, and decide which option you lean toward.

The worksheet asks, for each source, for the product, brand, and model, a working link, where you found it, and what kind of source it is. Then it asks what else you found and why these won.

Where to look

  • Manufacturer websites and spec sheets.
  • Retailer and supplier listings.
  • Trade publications and independent tests that compare equipment.
  • Videos that show the numbers. A tech or shop owner timing a job on camera, or a walkthrough of a machine's specs and cost. Check who is speaking and what they do in the trade.

Rules for good sources

  • Pick two brands of the same type of product. The same kind of machine from two different makers. These become the two options you give your supervisor in Week 3, where you compare them and say which one is best. For both options, collect price and the same three measures that matter most for your product, such as speed, capacity, durability, upkeep, or warranty. Do not pair a bare machine with a bundle that has extras.
  • A seller's page is good, but not alone. A manufacturer or seller page is a good source for what a product does and what it costs. It cannot be your only source, because anyone selling something has a reason to tell you that you need it. Pair it with an independent source, like an independent test.
  • Pick sources that connect to your problem. A spec only matters if it connects to the pattern you named.
  • Keep opinions out. If you catch yourself looking for "people say it is great," that belongs in Round Three.
  • Save the video link and note who is speaking. Copy the link to the video, not the channel, and write the speaker's name and role. In Week 3 you will watch it and pull out what they say.

Round Three: finding real voices

Round Three is qualitative data. That means real experience: reviews, forum posts, interviews, and accounts from people who have actually worked with this kind of equipment. Round Two will tell Ray what the machine does on paper. Round Three tells him what it is like to live with.

This week you find the voices and save where they came from. Find voices about each of your two Round Two options. Source 1 is about your Option 1 brand, and Source 2 is about your Option 2 brand. In Week 3 you open each one and dig into what they said.

The key word is independent. You need voices that are not selling you anything. A glowing description on the seller's own site is marketing, not a review.

Where to look

  • Trade forums and communities where working techs talk to each other.
  • Verified buyer reviews on sites that are not the seller's.
  • A real interview or conversation with someone who has used the equipment, like a tech, a service manager, or an instructor.
  • Videos from people in your trade. A tech or shop owner talking about using the equipment in a real shop. Someone in your industry who names who they are and what they do counts as a voice.

Ask who is speaking

A shop foreman with ten years of experience is different from an anonymous account with one post. Note who the voice is, so your reader knows how much weight to give it. In Week 3 you will also ask what each voice stands to gain or lose.

Step 1: Lock In Your Target

Your one-sentence ask: I am asking you to approve buying a second lift, around $5,300, so the shop is never down to one lift.

Your problem, with the incident that proves it: Last Tuesday, in the first rush of tire season, our only lift was down for six hours for a hydraulic fix. We turned away four jobs. With one 15-year-old lift booked about six hours a day, any day the lift is down or full, the work walks out the door.

Step 2: Build Your Theoretical Framework

Part 1: Find the pattern yourself

What is changing on the work side?: Winter tire season starts in October and our work doubles. More customers come in for tire swaps and seasonal checks, and every one of those jobs goes through the same lift.

What stays the same on the equipment side?: We still have one 15-year-old lift. It takes about 50 minutes per job and is booked about six hours a day. Nothing about the lift, the bays, or the way we run a job has changed. Only the number of cars has.

When did it build up?: It builds every season. Last year we were backed up almost every Saturday from October to January, and the backlog never fully cleared until the weather turned.

Is it a one-time thing?: No. The lift does not gain capacity between busy weeks, so the next busy week overflows the same way. Unless something about the lift changes, this will happen again every winter.

Part 2: Name the concept

Name of the concept: Theory of Constraints

Source title: Theory of Constraints (TOC) | Lean Production, Vorne Industries

Link: https://leanproduction.com/theory-of-constraints

Where found: My second search, "bottleneck one machine slows production," led to this page. My first search, "why shops fall behind," only returned general business advice, so I added the word bottleneck.

Kind of source: A company's lean production training page. The company may want to sell its products, so it is not a scholarly source, but it names the concept clearly. I use it to name the idea, not to prove my case.

Why it fits: Our one lift is the slowest step. Every job has to pass through it, so the whole shop can only finish as many jobs as the lift allows. The techs, the parts, and the customers are all ready, and everything waits on one machine. That is exactly what the concept describes.

Another reading: You could call this a staffing problem, but our techs are already standing around waiting for the lift, so more people would not fix it. You could also call it a scheduling problem, but even a perfect schedule cannot fit more than about six hours of jobs on one lift in a day.

What would prove it wrong: If the lift sat empty for hours each day and jobs still stacked up, the lift would not be the real limit. If the jobs we turn away were waiting on parts or techs instead of the lift, the concept would not apply.

The rules

  • Every source has a real, working link. If you cannot link to it, you cannot use it.
  • Save the link the moment you find it. Do not plan to find it again later. People lose good sources that way.
  • Check every link before you use it. Open it in a new tab. Does it load the page you describe?
  • AI can help you search, but every source has to be a real page you open and read yourself. AI tools can invent sources that look completely real, with an author, a title, and a believable name. A real-looking citation is not proof. If you cannot open it, it does not exist for this assignment.

If you get stuck

  • Change your search words. Try the key words from your framework and pattern, the name of the equipment type, or the name of the trade association in your field.
  • Try trade association sites, trade magazines, Google Scholar, and the ISU Library.
  • Look back at the section for the round you are in. Each one tells you what counts and what does not.
  • Ask your instructor. It is better to ask than to guess and use something weak.
